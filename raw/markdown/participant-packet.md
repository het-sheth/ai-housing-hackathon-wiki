Start Here
You're going to spend a weekend building an AI tool for one of the least glamorous and most
consequential problems in the country: why it is so hard to build housing people can afford.

You are not building a pitch deck, you are building a working prototype that someone at a planning
department, a nonprofit developer, or a community organization could actually open and use. You'll have
about thirty-nine hours, real public data, and access to people who do this work for a living.

## Page 4

Who Can Participate
•​ You must be 18 or older.
•​ College students of every kind — undergraduate, graduate, community college, bootcamp,
part-time, and recent graduates.
•​ Startup builders and early-stage teams.
•​ Anyone interested in solving problems related to housing.
•​ You don't need to be at a partner institution, in Pittsburgh, or in Pennsylvania. Participation is
virtual and open.
•​ Organizers, mentors, subject matter experts, judges, and sponsor representatives can't compete for
prizes.
Teams may mix — a student and a startup founder on the same team is fine, as long as everyone meets
the criteria and everyone actually participates.

The Three Challenge Tracks
Pick one. These came from people who work on housing in this region. Full briefs, with personas and
data sources, are published separately and worth reading before the weekend.

1. Development Feasibility Navigator
This challenge seeks an AI-driven tool that generates a 'Development Ease Score' for potential sites,
highlighting regulatory bottlenecks, required variances, and infrastructure gaps so cities and developers
can prioritize fast-track sites and identify areas needing policy or resource intervention.

Without a clear way to quantify these barriers, development pipelines remain slow, costly, and
unpredictable

2. Housing Production, Rents & Household Flow Observatory
An interactive observatory combining administrative, market, and community indicators to show how
the housing system changes over time.

Helps planners, policymakers, advocates, and journalists see what's actually happening before arguing
about what to do. The hard part is that the data doesn't agree with itself — different geographies, time
periods, and definitions of the same word.

3. Housing Typology, Equity & Climate Matchmaker
A decision-support tool matching locations with plausible housing types and surfacing tradeoffs across
demand, transit, equity, and climate resilience.

## Page 5

Helps municipalities decide what housing fits where, and community partners who want a say in that
answer. The hard part is that the tradeoffs genuinely conflict — a tool that pretends there's one right
answer is less useful than one showing you what you're giving up.

These datasets describe real neighborhoods and real households. The strongest submissions are explicit
about who benefits, who might be harmed, and what the tool gets wrong. "We don't have good data on
X, so our tool doesn't claim to answer it" is a strength, and judges score it that way.

## Page 6

Teams
•​ You register as an individual, not as a team.
•​ Teams are 1 to 5 people. Going solo is allowed, but three or four is where most good projects land.
•​ Teams form in Slack from September 19. Bring people you know or find them there.
•​ Every member has to actually participate. Adding a name to a roster isn't participating.

If You Don't Have a Team
1.​ Post in the team-formation channel when Slack opens: what you're good at, what you want to
learn, which track interests you, your time zone.
2.​ Reply to two other people's posts. Replying works considerably better than posting and waiting.

Four people with different skills beats five with the same one. The teams that do well usually have
someone comfortable with data, someone who can build an interface, and someone who can explain
why any of it matters to a housing practitioner. That third person is the one teams forget to recruit and
the one judges notice is missing.

Rules
Adapted from the Major League Hacking standard hackathon rules.

Building
1.​ All work on your project happens during the hackathon. Building starts at 9:00 a.m. Eastern on
Saturday, September 26 and stops at the submission deadline.
2.​ You can bring an idea. Thinking about the problem, sketching, reading the briefs, and exploring the
data beforehand are encouraged.
3.​ You can't bring code. Reusing your own prior project code isn't allowed, and neither is
open-sourcing something beforehand so you can use it during the event.
4.​ Your idea doesn't have to be original. If someone's built something similar, build yours anyway and
build it well.
5.​ You can use libraries, frameworks, open-source code, public datasets, and third-party APIs. List
them in your README.
6.​ When time is up, stop. You can fix a small bug you find while recording your demo. You can't add
features.

Code
1.​ Your code must be in a public repository, and it has to stay public after the event to remain eligible
for prizes.

## Page 7

2.​ Your commit history must be intact and start from kickoff. We check this — it's how the
no-prior-code rule is enforced rather than merely stated.
3.​ Strip your API keys and credentials before making the repo public. Every hackathon has at least one
team that doesn't.

Everything Else
1.​ One project per team. You can't submit the same project to another hackathon running at the same
time.
2.​ Everyone on the team participates.
3.​ Ask for help. Mentors, experts, organizers, and other teams are all fair game. Helping another team
is in the spirit of this, not against it.
4.​ Organizers can disqualify a team for breaking these rules or the code of conduct. This is rare and
we'd like it to stay that way.

AI Tools
Use them. This is an AI hackathon and it would be strange to ask otherwise. Coding assistants, code
generation, image generation, and large language models are all permitted.

Two conditions. Be honest about what you used. Be sure to list your AI tools in your submission and be
ready to answer questions about them. And build something: a thin wrapper around an existing AI
product isn't a project, and judges can tell the difference between a team that used AI to build faster and
a team that asked AI to build it.

Who Owns What You Build
You do. Entering doesn't transfer any ownership to the organizers, partners, or sponsors. We ask
permission to show your project publicly and that's the extent of it. If a partner wants to talk about
taking the work further, that conversation happens after the announcement and it's entirely yours to
accept or decline.

The Demo Video
The most underrated part of the submission. Judges watch it before they look at anything else, and some
will form most of their opinion from it. Three to five minutes.

•​ Start by saying the name of the hackathon and who you are.
•​ Show the thing working. A screen recording of the actual tool beats slides every time.
•​ Be honest about what's functional and what's mocked. Judges respect "this part works, this part is
a placeholder" far more than a smooth video that turns out to be a prototype of a prototype.
•​ Record it during the hackathon weekend, and keep it public afterward.
If you didn't finish, submit anyway and show what you built. An unfinished project that's honest about
where it stopped scores better than you'd expect, and considerably better than not submitting.

There are no late submissions. The form closes at 11:59 p.m. Sunday and we can't reopen it for one
team without being unfair to every team that made it.

Judging and Prizes
Judging is asynchronous, Monday September 28 through Wednesday September 30. Every project is
seen by at least three judges, so no single person determines your outcome. The highest-scoring
submissions win.

## Page 9

Judges come from housing practice, universities, technology, and the investment community. Some will
know your track's subject matter deeply and some will be evaluating your technical work. This is why
explaining your project clearly matters as much as building it well.

Evaluation Criteria
Problem Value​
Does the prototype address a costly, frequent, or consequential housing bottleneck identified in the
challenge briefs (e.g., development feasibility, missing-middle housing typologies, or policy/permitting
navigation)?

User Fit & Usability​
Is the interface intuitive, plain-language, and practical for target end-users (such as municipal planners,
small developers, nonprofits, or community partners) to adopt?

Technical Execution​
Does the working demo/prototype reliably perform its core functional tasks during testing and
presentation?

Data & AI Integrity​
Are sources, statutes, and baseline assumptions accurately cited and grounded? Are privacy constraints
(no PII/sensitive data) and model uncertainties handled responsibly? Is there a clear
human-in-the-loop/escalation path for consequential decisions?

Actionability​
Does the output directly assist or accelerate real-world decisions in housing development, policy,
planning, or permitting rather than merely presenting abstract analytics?

Continuation Potential​
Is there a credible path to post-event testing, maintenance, community ownership, or pilot adoption by
public/civic partners (e.g., Allegheny County, City Planning, URA, PHFA)?

MANDATORY ELIGIBILITY & COMPLIANCE CHECKS:

• Originality: Must be a new, ground-up build created during the hackathon window (no pre-existing
products/pitches).

• Required Deliverables: Working code repository, demo video (3-5 min), documentation, data/source
citations, and limitations statement.

• Responsible Framing: Tool must be positioned strictly as decision support, not binding legal, financial,
or zoning advice.

Questions People Actually Ask
I've never been to a hackathon. Is this going to be over my head?
No. A substantial share of participants are first-timers. Workshops start from the basics, mentors are
there specifically for people who are stuck, and there's a channel for questions you think are too basic to
ask. They aren't.

Do I have to stay up all night?
No, and we'd rather you didn't. Sleep-deprived teams make bad decisions and ship worse projects.
Nothing in the judging rewards suffering.

Can I work on an idea I've already been thinking about?
Yes. Ideas are fine, encouraged even. Code isn't. Start the implementation Saturday morning.

What if my project doesn't work by Sunday?
Submit it anyway and be straight about where it stopped. That scores better than not submitting, which
scores zero.

Do I need to know anything about housing?
Not on Friday. You'll need to know something by Sunday night, which is what the office hours and
challenge briefs are for. Reading the briefs beforehand puts you well ahead.

Do I need to use any specific tools or platform?
No, but we are providing Cursor tokens.

What if I can only participate part of the weekend?

Participate anyway. Tell your team upfront what your availability is. Teams handle this fine when it's
stated in advance and badly when it's a surprise on Sunday.

Can I keep working on this afterward?
Yes, and several teams do. It's your project. Past hackathon projects have turned into capstones, class
projects, and companies.
