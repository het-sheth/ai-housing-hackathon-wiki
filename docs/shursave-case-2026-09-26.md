# Bloomfield ShurSave: proposal changes and decision points

Date: 2026-09-26. Public-source case review prompted by Jack Billings's example. This is a historical reconstruction, not an interview, a legal determination or proof that our prototype would have changed the outcome.

## What happened

| When | Documented event | Relevance to a proposal comparison |
|---|---|---|
| 2018-2019 | An earlier apartment proposal without a grocery store met community opposition. Community planning then emphasized a grocery store and housing, including affordable housing. | Program value is part of the choice. A housing proposal cannot be judged only by its zoning friction. |
| 2021-2022 | Echo Realty publicly described a grocery-anchored concept with about 190 apartments. For the related rezoning of 234 Ella Street, the Planning Commission recommended approval with future Site Plan Review and a Development Activities Meeting required for development involving that parcel. A neighborhood account says City Council approved the rezoning in April 2022. | A smaller concept existed, but the zoning-map change was a separate approval and did not approve a building. The public material reviewed does not establish the concept's complete zoning path, cost or viability. It should not be labeled approved or by-right. |
| August-November 2023 | Echo submitted a six-story mixed-use proposal with 248 apartments. The City's hearing agenda listed a grocery special exception, a floor-area-ratio request from 2:1 to 3.25:1, a height request from 45 feet and three stories to 75 feet and six stories, and residential-adjacency height issues. The later written decision in the appeal file describes the FAR request as 3.1:1. The Zoning Board denied the FAR and height variances, including residential-compatibility relief, while granting the grocery special exception. | The significant barrier attached to the particular building program, not a verdict that the land could not be used. Different requested relief had different outcomes. The FAR figures also show that proposal versions must be dated. |
| December 2023-July 2024 | The developer appealed. In July, the neighborhood organization reported that Giant Eagle would acquire the property and continue the grocery store, with no housing added at that time. A 2025 City Controller report describes the redevelopment proposal as withdrawn in 2024. | The observed fallback was grocery continuity without the proposed homes. It was not a documented lower-rise housing approval. |

The earlier 190-unit concept may be the most useful missing comparison. A neighborhood article says the earlier plan did not require the later variances, but that claim has not been checked against a complete City application or a zoning decision. In particular, the City minutes document only a recommendation on the Ella Street map change, with future review required. It is also unclear why the concept grew to 248 units. Echo publicly linked the later height to project costs and the affordable-unit requirement, and its appeal argued that topography and subsurface conditions made code-compliant redevelopment unusually costly. The Board disagreed that the evidence established the requested variances and found the asserted hardship more connected to the scale of the proposed development than unique site conditions. None of these positions supplies a public pro forma or proves whether the 190-unit concept could pencil.

## What this case tests

A responsible scenario report might put the 2021 concept and the 2023 submitted proposal side by side, identify the exact change in height, floor area, unit count, affordability commitment and approval path, and mark cost and revenue assumptions as missing. It should also show the grocery-only outcome separately as an observed result. Calling the latter "easier" without showing zero new homes would hide the team's housing objective.

The case cautions against a single parcel-level Development Ease Score. The 2023 proposal encountered a major zoning barrier while the grocery use continued. A red site rating would obscure that distinction; a green rating could obscure the unapproved housing plan. The useful unit of analysis is a site plus a specified proposal, with each requested approval and unknown shown separately. This is a product implication, not a new scoring decision.

The public record reviewed does not show a full pro forma, site engineering study, the complete disposition of the appeal or a verified lower-rise design. We therefore cannot conclude that a tool would have predicted the outcome, that variance denial alone caused the sale, or that a specific alternate housing scheme was viable. This large mixed-use project also does not validate demand for the prototype's single-home repair-versus-expansion comparison.

For a practitioner follow-up, the most useful question is which decision would have changed if the 190-unit and 248-unit paths had been compared earlier: choosing a program, deciding whether to seek variances, or testing whether grocery plus affordable homes could work with fewer units. Ask what evidence was actually available at that point and who assembled it.
